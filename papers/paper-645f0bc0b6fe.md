## Summary points

- The study combines three sources—about 360,000 interactions classified as scientific from 15 million anonymized Gemini interactions, an inventory of 2,690 specialized scientific AI models with roughly 460,000 citations, and a survey of 637 US and UK scientists—to map AI adoption onto shared scientific task taxonomies.
- The authors find that scientists use general-purpose AI disproportionately often and report saving just under seven hours per week on average; LLMs mainly support broad activities such as coding and writing, whereas specialized models focus on domain-specific prediction, generation, and simulation.
- The reported gains may shift bottlenecks rather than remove them: respondents describe more untested hypotheses, substantial effort spent verifying AI outputs, and movement toward safer research questions. Key limitations include a non-representative self-reported survey and imperfect task classification, whose exact-match accuracy falls to 28% at the most granular MIT taxonomy level.

## Main takeaway

AI appears to be widely adopted and productivity-enhancing in scientific work, with general and specialized models serving complementary roles, but validation capacity, classification uncertainty, and non-representative survey evidence limit conclusions about its overall effect on scientific progress.
